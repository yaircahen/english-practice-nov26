# Actionable Gamification: Beyond Points, Badges, and Leaderboards — Working Reference

Author: Yu-kai Chou (2015; revised ed. 2019). This is a paraphrased working reference of the book's
ideas, written so it can be applied to design work. It is not the book's text.

---

## 1. Core thesis

- **Gamification = Human-Focused Design.** Most systems are *Function-Focused*: built to get the job
  done efficiently, assuming people will do what they're told. Games are the one discipline that has
  spent decades optimising purely for human motivation, because a game has no reason to exist other
  than making people want to play it. Gamification is lifting those lessons into non-game contexts.
- **The PBL Fallacy.** Points, Badges and Leaderboards are the *shell* of a game, not the reason it's
  fun. Bolting PBLs onto a boring process ("chocolate-covered broccoli") produces short-lived novelty
  and then resentment. Chou's rule: start with the *feeling* you want the user to have (proud, curious,
  anxious, part of something), then pick mechanics; never the reverse.
- **Everything is Core Drives.** If an action has none of the 8 Core Drives behind it, no one does it.
  All motivation, in games or life, is some mix of the 8.
- **Desired Actions and Win-States.** Design work is: define the specific actions you want users to
  take, and give each a clear win-state (the moment the user knows they succeeded).
- **Three Octalysis levels** (five exist; the book teaches I–III):
  - Level I: map the 8 Core Drives of a single experience on the octagon.
  - Level II: do it across the **4 Experience Phases** (Discovery, Onboarding, Scaffolding, Endgame).
  - Level III: add **player types** (e.g. Bartle's) as a third axis.

## 2. The Octalysis octagon — the 8 Core Drives

Numbering and placement on the octagon matter (see §3).

| # | Core Drive | One-line meaning | Typical feeling |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Epic Meaning & Calling** | I'm part of something bigger than me, or I was "chosen" | Purpose, pride |
| 2 | **Development & Accomplishment** | I'm making progress, gaining skill, overcoming challenge | Competence |
| 3 | **Empowerment of Creativity & Feedback** | I try things, see results, and adjust; the activity is its own reward | Flow, agency |
| 4 | **Ownership & Possession** | It's mine, so I want to improve it and get more | Attachment |
| 5 | **Social Influence & Relatedness** | Mentorship, acceptance, competition, envy, companionship, nostalgia | Belonging |
| 6 | **Scarcity & Impatience** | I want it because I can't have it (yet) | Craving |
| 7 | **Unpredictability & Curiosity** | I don't know what happens next, so I keep paying attention | Suspense |
| 8 | **Loss & Avoidance** | I act to avoid losing something or a negative outcome | Fear, urgency |

Hidden ninth drive: **Sensation** (physical pleasure/pain), acknowledged but not on the octagon.

### Core Drive 1 — Epic Meaning & Calling
- The user believes the activity serves something beyond self-interest, or that they are uniquely
  suited to it. Weak on day-to-day urgency, strong on long-term loyalty and willingness to do hard work.
- Examples in the book: Wikipedia contributors (no pay, huge effort); Waze drivers reporting hazards;
  Freerice; Kickstarter backers; Apple's early "think different" tribe; brand mythologies.
- Game techniques (Chou's numbered catalogue; numbers as given in the book where I'm confident):
  - **Narrative (#10)** — a story that frames why the action matters.
  - **Humanity Hero (#26)** — your action helps people/the planet (Freerice, charity ties).
  - **Elitism (#27)** — belonging to a group that is "better" (schools, nations, teams).
  - **Beginner's Luck (#23)** — the user gets an early, unusually good outcome that makes them feel chosen.
  - **Free Lunch (#15)** — a gift given "just because", creating a sense of being favoured.
  - **Destiny Child (#92)** — the user is told they were chosen/fated for this.
  - **Creationist** — build something that others will use (also touches CD3/CD4).

### Core Drive 2 — Development & Accomplishment
- The easiest drive to design for and the one PBLs target. Only works when the challenge is real:
  a badge for nothing is worthless; a badge for something hard is a trophy.
- Examples: eBay feedback stars; LinkedIn profile completeness; Khan Academy; Foursquare mayorships;
  Duolingo streaks and levels; Nike+ goals.
- Techniques:
  - **Status Points (#1)** — visible score tracking progress (vs Exchangeable Points, which are CD4).
  - **Achievement Symbols (#2)** — badges, medals, titles; must map to real accomplishment.
  - **Leaderboards (#3)** — only motivate those near the top; use micro-leaderboards (friends,
    this week, this group) so most users see a reachable gap. "Urgent optimism" vs demoralisation.
  - **Progress Bars (#4)** — LinkedIn's completeness bar; works via the Zeigarnik effect and CD8.
  - **Quest Lists (#5)** — clear next tasks with win-states.
  - **Step-by-Step Overlay Tutorial** — guided first actions (onboarding).
  - **Boss Fights** — a defining challenge that tests everything learned so far.
  - **Aura Effect / Anticipation Parade / High Five / Rockstar Effect** — recognition moments
    that make an accomplishment visible and celebrated.

### Core Drive 3 — Empowerment of Creativity & Feedback
- The "evergreen" drive: the user keeps combining things, gets feedback, and iterates. LEGO,
  painting, chess, Minecraft, deck-building. Once designed well, it needs no new content to stay fun.
  Chou calls this the holy grail of long-term engagement (Endgame).
- Requires: meaningful choices (not "poison picker" fake choices), fast feedback, room to express style.
- Examples: Minecraft; Candy Crush boosters; Plants vs Zombies plant selection; Magic: The Gathering;
  Chou's own "Octalysis Prime" style open-ended goals.
- Techniques:
  - **Milestone Unlocks (#19)** — new tools/abilities unlock at milestones, widening the choice space.
  - **Boosters (#31)** — temporary power-ups that reward creative timing.
  - **Real-Time Control (#59)** — the user acts and sees the consequence immediately.
  - **Chain Combos** — multiplying effects for sequencing actions well.
  - **Instant Feedback** — the loop that makes experimentation satisfying.
  - **Poison Picker / Choice Perception (#89)** — the illusion of choice; Chou warns against relying on it.
  - **Plant Picker / Meaningful Choices** — choices with real trade-offs and no single dominant option.
  - **Blank Fills** — give the user something to complete rather than consume.
  - **Evergreen Mechanics / Evergreen Combos** — systems where combinations stay fresh indefinitely.

### Core Drive 4 — Ownership & Possession
- Owning something (points, virtual goods, an avatar, data, a position) makes people protective of
  it and eager to grow it. Links to the endowment effect and the IKEA effect.
- Examples: FarmVille farms; Pokémon ("gotta catch 'em all"); Pinterest boards; frequent-flyer
  points; customised avatars; Dropbox space earned via referrals.
- Techniques:
  - **Build-From-Scratch (#43)** — users who assemble it value it more (IKEA effect).
  - **Collection Sets (#16)** — incomplete sets drive completion (also CD6/CD8).
  - **Exchangeable Points** — a currency the user accumulates and spends (distinct from Status Points).
  - **Avatar** — a self-representation to invest in.
  - **Earned Lunch** — a fixed reward for a fixed action, the user feels they earned.
  - **Virtual Goods** — items that are "mine".
  - **Monitor Attachment** — the more someone watches a metric, the more they feel it is theirs.
  - **Protector Quest / Recruiter Quest** — defend what you own, bring others in.
  - **The Alfred Effect (#83)** — a system that has learned the user so well (like Batman's butler)
    that leaving means losing the fit; strong retention via CD4 and CD8.

### Core Drive 5 — Social Influence & Relatedness
- Everything social: mentorship, acceptance, social proof, competition, envy, companionship,
  nostalgia and relatedness (we bond with things that remind us of people/places).
- Examples: Facebook; Dropbox referrals; LinkedIn connection counts; Trophy shelves on profiles;
  Zynga's "ask friends for help"; Duolingo leaderboards; Nike+ sharing runs.
- Techniques:
  - **Mentorship (#61)** — veterans guide newcomers (serves the Endgame for veterans too).
  - **Brag Buttons (#57)** — one-click sharing of an accomplishment.
  - **Water Coolers (#58)** — a place for off-task conversation that builds community.
  - **Group Quests (#29)** — goals only reachable together.
  - **Social Prod (#62)** — low-effort nudges (poke, like, wave) that start interactions.
  - **Social Treasures (#63)** — rewards only another user can give you.
  - **Trophy Shelves (#64)** — a visible display of achievements and possessions.
  - **Touting (#71)** — users promote the system because it reflects well on them.
  - **Conformity Anchors** — showing what "people like you" do (social norms).
  - **Thank-You Economy / Friending** — gratitude and connection loops.

### Core Drive 6 — Scarcity & Impatience
- Wanting something more because it is rare, limited, or not yet available. Chou's "Appointment
  Dynamics" and timed gates are the signature mechanics.
- Examples: Facebook's college-by-college launch and Google+ invites (exclusivity); Woot's one-deal-
  a-day; Tesla waitlists; "Come back in 2 hours" in FarmVille; Candy Crush lives; limited editions.
- Techniques:
  - **Appointment Dynamics (#21)** — come back at a set time for a reward.
  - **Dangling (#44)** — show something desirable the user can see but can't have yet.
  - **Prize Pacing** — rewards doled out gradually (collect 7 pieces over 7 days).
  - **Options Pacing** — options unlocked over time rather than all at once.
  - **Count Down Timers (#65)** — visible clock to a deadline or availability window.
  - **Torture Breaks (#66)** — forced pauses that make returning feel like relief (Candy Crush lives).
  - **Moats (#67)** — barriers between the user and the goal that make the goal more attractive.
  - **Magnetic Caps (#68)** — limits (e.g. "max 5 per day") that increase desire.
  - **Evolved UI / Anchored Juxtaposition** — showing both the paid and the hard path side by side.

### Core Drive 7 — Unpredictability & Curiosity
- Not knowing what happens next; the Skinner-box drive. Responsible for gambling, lotteries,
  loot boxes, slot machines, and also for curiosity, surprise, and good storytelling.
- Examples: Skinner's variable-ratio schedules; Woot; Magic boosters; Google doodles and Easter
  eggs; the Swedish Speed Camera Lottery (slow drivers enter a lottery funded by speeders' fines);
  Candy Crush random rewards; Twitter's feed; mystery boxes.
- Techniques:
  - **Glowing Choice (#28)** — highlight the one obvious next thing to click (onboarding).
  - **Mini Quests** — small unexpected side tasks.
  - **Visual Storytelling** — curiosity about what comes next in the narrative.
  - **Easter Eggs / Sudden Rewards (#30)** — surprise rewards not announced in advance.
  - **Random Rewards (#72)** — "mystery box": reward is certain, content is not.
  - **Obvious Wonder (#73)** — something strange enough to demand exploration.
  - **Rolling Rewards (#74)** — lottery: someone wins every round, odds improve as others leave.
  - **Sweepstakes / Lottery** — probabilistic prizes.

### Core Drive 8 — Loss & Avoidance
- People work harder to avoid losing than to gain (Kahneman/Tversky loss aversion). Also covers
  avoiding fading opportunities and admitting that past effort was wasted.
- Examples: FarmVille crops withering; eBay auctions ("you've been outbid"); streak loss in Duolingo;
  deadlines; "only 3 left"; sunk-cost loyalty in MMOs; sticking with the default.
- Techniques:
  - **Sunk Cost Prison (#50)** — the more invested, the harder to leave even if unhappy.
  - **Progress Loss** — the threat that earned progress will decay or vanish.
  - **Rightful Heritage** — show the user something as "already theirs" so losing it hurts.
  - **Evanescent Opportunity** — an offer that disappears if not acted on now.
  - **Status Quo Sloth (#85)** — people avoid changing defaults; design defaults in your favour.
  - **Scarlet Letter** — visible marks of failure or inactivity.
  - **Visual Grave** — a view of what's dead/lost so far.
  - **The Weep Tune** — sound/visual feedback that makes a loss feel real.
  - **FOMO Punch** — fear of missing what others get.
  - **Countdown Timers** (shared with CD6).

## 3. Reading the octagon — the two axes

### Left Brain vs Right Brain (extrinsic vs intrinsic)
- **Left Brain (extrinsic):** CD2 Accomplishment, CD4 Ownership, CD6 Scarcity. Logic, calculation,
  acquiring things. The user wants a *result*.
- **Right Brain (intrinsic):** CD3 Creativity, CD5 Social, CD7 Curiosity. The *activity itself*
  is the reward: creating, connecting, discovering.
- CD1 and CD8 sit on the vertical axis and belong to neither side.
- The labels are symbolic, not neuroscience.
- Key warning: the **overjustification effect** (Deci; Lepper & Greene). Paying for something people
  already enjoyed turns it into work, and when the pay stops, so does the activity. Daniel Pink's
  *Drive* and Self-Determination Theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness) are cited.
- Design rule: extrinsic drives are good for *getting people in the door* (Discovery/Onboarding);
  design must then transition to intrinsic drives for Scaffolding/Endgame, or engagement decays
  into a grind that only continues while rewards escalate.

### White Hat vs Black Hat
- **White Hat (top):** CD1 Meaning, CD2 Accomplishment, CD3 Creativity. Users feel powerful,
  fulfilled, in control. Weakness: **no urgency**. People feel good but may never act *now*.
- **Black Hat (bottom):** CD6 Scarcity, CD7 Unpredictability, CD8 Loss. Create urgency and
  obsession. Weakness: users feel manipulated, lose a sense of control, and quit with a bad taste
  once they can.
- CD4 and CD5 are in the middle and can swing either way.
- Not a moral judgement: people deliberately use Black Hat on themselves (gym commitments, diets).
  It's about *how it feels*, not whether it's ethical. Ethics depends on whether the Desired Action
  genuinely benefits the user.
- Design rule: use Black Hat to trigger the action, then **hand off to White Hat** so people stay
  because they want to. Zynga is the cautionary case: all Black Hat, huge spikes, then collapse as
  users felt used and left. Companies that fail with gamification often never move beyond
  Black Hat + PBLs.

## 4. Level II — the 4 Experience Phases

Each phase has its own Desired Actions and Win-States; a Level II analysis draws one octagon per
phase (8 drives × 4 phases = a 32-cell design grid).

1. **Discovery** — Why would someone even start? Before first use. Lean on CD1 (a compelling
   "why"), CD7 (curiosity, mystery), CD5 (word of mouth, social proof), CD6 (invite-only, waitlists).
2. **Onboarding** — Learning the rules and tools. First session(s). CD2 (quick early wins), CD7
   (Glowing Choice, surprises), CD1 (Beginner's Luck), Step-by-step tutorials, "Win-State fast".
   Plants vs Zombies is the model onboarding: teach by doing, one mechanic at a time.
3. **Scaffolding** — The repeated daily journey toward goals; most of the user's lifetime. CD4
   (something to build), CD5 (community), CD6/CD8 (Appointment Dynamics, streaks, loss of progress),
   CD2 (visible progress). Where habits form.
4. **Endgame** — Keeping veterans who have "seen everything". CD3 (evergreen creativity), CD5
   (mentorship, status), CD1 (legacy, meaning). Most products never design for this and bleed their
   best users. World of Warcraft raids/PvP; Blizzard's endgame content are the reference cases.

Rule of thumb: Discovery and Onboarding may be extrinsic/Black Hat heavy; Scaffolding and Endgame
must shift to intrinsic/White Hat.

## 5. Level III — player types

- Bartle's four MUD types: **Achievers, Explorers, Socializers, Killers**. Chou uses them as one
  possible axis but notes Bartle himself says they don't transfer cleanly to gamification.
- Marczewski's Hexad (Philanthropist, Socialiser, Free Spirit, Achiever, Player, Disruptor) is
  mentioned as an alternative.
- Chou's position: a person's "type" shifts by phase and context, so design for drives first and
  types second.

## 6. Six contextual types of rewards

Rewards aren't good or bad in themselves; their *context* decides which drive they trigger.

| Reward type | Mechanic | Main drive |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed-Action Rewards | Earned Lunch: do X, get Y | CD4 / CD2 |
| Random Rewards | Mystery Box: do X, get something unknown | CD7 |
| Sudden Rewards | Easter Eggs: unexpected reward for an action | CD7 |
| Rolling Rewards | Lottery: chance to win every cycle | CD7 / CD6 |
| Social Treasure | Gifting: only others can give it | CD5 |
| Prize Pacing | Collection Set: pieces over time | CD4 / CD6 |

## 7. Other frameworks explained through Octalysis (ch. 15)

- **Fogg Behavior Model** (B = Motivation × Ability × Trigger): Octalysis is the Motivation part;
  Ability = reduce friction ("anti-core-drives"); Triggers = feedback mechanics.
- **Self-Determination Theory** (autonomy → CD3, competence → CD2, relatedness → CD5).
- **Daniel Pink, Drive** (autonomy → CD3, mastery → CD2, purpose → CD1).
- **Nicole Lazzaro's 4 Keys to Fun** (Easy fun → CD7/CD3, Hard fun → CD2, People fun → CD5,
  Serious fun → CD1/CD4).
- **Csikszentmihalyi's Flow** — challenge/skill balance maps to CD2 + CD3 in the Scaffolding phase.
- **Kahneman / behavioural economics** — loss aversion (CD8), endowment (CD4), scarcity (CD6).
- **Nir Eyal's Hooked** (trigger, action, variable reward, investment) maps to CD7 + CD4.
- **Bartle types** — see §5.

## 8. Designing from scratch — the Octalysis Strategy Dashboard (ch. 17)

Before touching mechanics, fill in five boxes, in order:

1. **Business Metrics** (Game Objectives) — what measurable outcome the project exists for.
2. **Users** (Players) — who they are, what they already want, what drives them.
3. **Desired Actions** (Win-States) — the specific behaviours, ordered across the 4 phases, that
   move the metrics. Each must have a clear win-state.
4. **Feedback Mechanics** (Triggers) — how the user is told they've progressed or must act
   (progress bars, notifications, sounds, status changes).
5. **Incentives** (Rewards) — what they get, chosen from the six reward types above.

Then: run the **Octalysis Brainstorm** — for each phase and each Core Drive, ask "how could this
drive push the user to the Desired Action here?" (the 32-cell grid). Score the octagon, look for
flat sides, and especially check whether the design has (a) any urgency at all, (b) any intrinsic
drive to carry the Endgame.

Common failure modes Chou calls out:
- All PBLs and no Core Drives beyond CD2.
- No Discovery design (great product nobody tries).
- Great Onboarding, nothing for Scaffolding (people finish the tutorial and leave).
- No Endgame (veterans churn).
- All Black Hat (spike then collapse, brand damage).
- Rewarding the wrong Desired Action (metrics go up, value goes down).

## 9. Lifestyle Gamification (later chapters / Chou's "4 steps")

Applying the same drives to your own life: pick a "game" (a goal with Win-States), identify your
skills/stats, find your "quests" (daily Desired Actions), find allies (CD5), and keep an eye on
which drives are carrying you so you don't rely only on Black Hat deadlines.

## 11. Quick design checklist (distilled)

1. What is the Desired Action, and what is its Win-State?
2. Which phase is the user in? (Discovery / Onboarding / Scaffolding / Endgame)
3. Which of the 8 drives currently push toward the action? Which sides of the octagon are flat?
4. Is there any urgency (Black Hat)? Is there any reason to stay once urgency fades (White Hat)?
5. Is the long-term engine extrinsic (will decay) or intrinsic (CD3/CD5/CD7, evergreen)?
6. Does the feedback loop make progress visible within seconds, not days?
7. Would the user feel used if they understood the design? If yes, redesign.
